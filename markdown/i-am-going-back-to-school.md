I'm going back to school! I studied Electronics and Communication Engineering as an undergraduate, went straight into software engineering after graduating. I have spent the last six years learning on the job. Now, I'm heading back to the classroom. This time for a master's in computer science through [Georgia Tech's OMSCS program](https://www.omscs.gatech.edu/).

## Why a master's degree?

It's been a little over six years since I started working as a software engineer, and I've grown into a lead engineering role. But I have to admit that much of my learning so far has been self-directed.

Whenever discussions turn to fundamental computer science topics, I sometimes find myself dealing with imposter syndrome.[^1] I end up going back to blogs, textbooks, and other resources to build the understanding I need to contribute confidently.

Throughout my career, I've felt that my understanding of computer science fundamentals is incomplete. Self-learning has helped me get this far, but I want a more structured way to revisit these topics, fill in the gaps, and develop a deeper understanding of the field.

I graduated with a GPA of 2.67/4.0 in my undergraduate degree.[^2] There could be plenty of reasons for that. Perhaps I was more interested in learning to program, building robotics projects, and participating in hackathons. In hindsight, though, those might just be excuses.

I wish I had taken my studies more seriously back then. It is what it is. Pursuing a master's now feels like getting a second chance, an opportunity to approach formal education with a different mindset and make the most of it this time.

Another factor is the uncertainty surrounding the future of software engineering in the age of AI. For years, I've been writing APIs, scaling systems, and training models. AI is already changing how much of this work gets done, and that trend is unlikely to slow down.

I don't think a master's degree is a guarantee of staying relevant, nor do I think formal education is the only way to build strong fundamentals. But I believe a deeper understanding of computer science will help me reason about problems, evaluate solutions, and adapt as the tools we use continue to evolve.

## Why now?

For these reasons, I've been debating whether to pursue a master's degree for the past two or three years. But I also have my own reasons for making this decision now rather than earlier — or perhaps later.

I started my career at a high-velocity startup, where I had to learn new skills while keeping up with the pace of work. That was probably one of the reasons I didn't pursue a master's earlier.

When I moved to Target and settled into my new role after a few months, the desire to formalize my education started resurfacing. I'm happy with my career trajectory, have a great set of people to work with, and enjoy relatively stable working arrangements. **_(knocks on wood.)_**

I also feel that my professional experience and the systems I've worked on at scale have prepared me well for graduate-level study. I'm hoping that experience will help me connect the theoretical concepts I learn to the engineering problems I encounter at work.

## Choosing the Program

I had a fairly clear idea of what I wanted from a program. An on-campus master's in India wasn't particularly appealing to me. Preparing for entrance exams such as GATE and stepping away from work would mean putting my career and income on hold for a couple of years.

That left me looking at online programs. I'd known about [Georgia Tech's OMSCS program](https://www.omscs.gatech.edu/) since my undergraduate days, and its affordability had always stood out to me. I also considered UT Austin's MSCSO and Illinois' online MCS.

All three were compelling options. I ultimately chose OMSCS for the combination of flexibility, affordability, and Georgia Tech's reputation in the industry.

## Applying to the program
The application process turned out to be surprisingly straightforward.

I took the TOEFL in June 2026 to meet the English proficiency requirement. Later, I learned that my undergrad degree mentions English as the medium of instruction should have been enough to satisfy that requirement, so the test may not have been necessary.

In July, I finalized my three recommenders; two colleagues from work and one undergraduate professor. By August 1st, I had prepared drafts for my recommenders to review and agree on.

I submitted my application on August 4th, sent my TOEFL scores, and had all three recommendations submitted by August 7th. On August 12th, I received my acceptance!!!

My GPA was a concern, but I had been candid in the application about my academic performance and the reasons behind it. I can only speculate about how much my recommendations and statement of purpose contributed to the outcome.

On September 1st, I received institute admission, with the remaining task being getting my official degree and transcripts sent to Georgia tech directly by my University. I requested electronic copies to be sent from my university on September 18th, and Georgia Tech confirmed receipt on October 1st.

And that was pretty much it. The process was much smoother than I'd expected.

## Getting ready for OMSCS

I have decided to brush up on my math skills. After all, it has been close to 6 years since I graduated and I can already feel my math skills are rusty. I have started reading a lot of books on computer science lately especially around OS, Computer Network, Compilers and more. [^3]


## Busy studying from Spring 2027 onwards
And that's that! I'll be heading back to school in January 2027, balancing graduate coursework with a full-time engineering career.

I'm excited to see what I learn, what challenges me, and how this experience shapes the way I approach engineering.

Wish me luck!
